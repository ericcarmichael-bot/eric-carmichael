# Research Brief: Jones Act Competition and Hawaiʻi Freight Rates

## The Challenge

Hawaii is largely dependent on ocean transportation for the majority of goods moving between the islands and the continental United States. While many goods will move via air-freight, staple items are largely transported via ocean cargo. Under current US shipping laws, domestic goods that move between domestic ports, must be transported on vessels that are domestic flagged. This limits which cargo companies can operate Hawaii to mainland or mainland to Hawaii shipping routes.

The economic question at play here is whether or not these restrictions materially affect freight rates and therefore the on-shelf price of products sold here on the islands. Hawaii's cargo shipments are primarily served by two companies that are both US flagged: Matson and Pasha. There are a few foreign companies that currently serve Hawaii directly as well, bringing international cargo straight to the islands: Ocean Network Express and CMA CGM (the world's third-largest container shipping company).

 This creates a useful counterfactual: if foreign carriers that already participate in trade throughout the Pacific were allowed to also carry domestic cargo between Honolulu and the West Coast, would the additional competition and possibly better route efficiencies reduce freight rates?

## Research Question

Does available evidence support the hypothesis that eliminating the Jones Act, specifically the prohibition on foreign-flagged carriers from transporting domestic cargo between Honolulu and the U.S West Coast, would reduce ocean container rates by increasing competition and breaking apart the existing oligopoly shared by Pasha and Matson?

## Initial Hypothesis

I expect that allowing additional carriers to compete for domestic Hawaii cargo would create downward pricing pressure on freight rates. Increasing the competition beyond the existing oligopoly would require the existing companies to find efficiencies as well as allow foreign vessels to compete.

My initial reasoning is that the current law greatly limits the number of carriers in the domestic market. Cargo companies have substantial barriers to entry to overcome (cost of ships, regulatory hurdles, etc). Removing the restriction would not necessarily mean a large influx in companies able to move cargo but would expand the set of carriers that are even capable of doing so.

## Legal and Market Setting

46 U.S.C. §55102 generally prohibits transportation of merchandise between U.S. points unless the vessel satisfies coastwise requirements. These requirements generally are: US built ship, US citizenship for the owner or 75% ownership by US citizens. 

Matson identifies Pasha Hawaii as its primary U.S.-flagged Jones Act competitor in Hawaiʻi and identifies foreign-flag carriers including Ocean Network Express and CMA CGM as competitors for foreign-origin Hawaiʻi cargo. That distinction matters: foreign carriers may serve Hawaiʻi's international trade but ordinarily cannot carry domestic Honolulu–West Coast cargo.

## Economic Mechanisms

### Barriers to Entry

Coastwise requirements restrict which vessels may compete for domestic cargo. Removing that constraint would enlarge the set of legally eligible suppliers, although legal eligibility would not guarantee actual entry, as there are high fixed costs.

### Competition and Market Structure

Domestic container service to Hawaiʻi is concentrated among relatively few operators. Standard microeconomic theory predicts that reducing entry barriers can increase competitive pressure, but the size of any price effect depends on costs, capacity, service differentiation, and whether new firms would actually enter. ### Fixed Costs and Capacity Utilization

Ocean shipping has large fixed costs and lower marginal costs for filling otherwise-unused vessel capacity. A carrier already calling Honolulu as part of an international network could evaluate incremental domestic cargo differently from a carrier whose service is dedicated principally to domestic Hawaiʻi routes.

### Directional Imbalance

Published Matson household-goods tariffs show substantial directional differences for a standardized 40-foot container:

| Tariff year | West Coast → Honolulu ocean rate | Honolulu → West Coast ocean rate | Dollar gap | Westbound premium |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2025 | $7,276 | $4,254 | $3,022 | 71.0% |
| 2026 | $7,476 | $4,354 | $3,122 | 71.7% |

Including Honolulu wharfage leaves the dollar gaps unchanged because the same wharfage applies by equipment size in both directions. The percentage premium becomes approximately 65.7% in 2025 and 66.2% in 2026.

These are **published household-goods tariff rates**, not average market rates for all containerized freight. They should be used as standardized directional evidence rather than as a claimed market-wide Jones Act premium.

## Preliminary Evidence Supporting the Mechanism

1. The domestic Hawaiʻi trade has effectively two main competitors. 
2. Foreign-flag carriers already physically serve Oʻahu with international cargo but are unable to compete for domestic cargo.
3. Published Matson rates display a large, persistent directional difference.
4. Matson identifies changes to the Jones Act as a competitive risk that could allow lower-cost competitors to enter its Hawaiʻi market.

The fourth point supports the plausibility of the entry mechanism derived from Matson's own SEC disclosure.

## Falsification Tests

The hypothesis would be weakened if:

1. Comparable foreign-carrier rates, after adjusting for distance, service, and surcharges, are not materially below domestic Hawaiʻi rates.
2. Domestic rates closely track measurable operating costs rather than competitive conditions.
3. Additional eligible capacity exists but does not result in lower offered rates.
4. Directional differences disappear after controlling for demand and equipment repositioning.
5. Potential foreign entrants indicate that the route would not be economically attractive even if coastwise restrictions were relaxed.
