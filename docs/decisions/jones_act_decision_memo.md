# Decision Memo — Jones Act and Hawaiʻi Freight Rates

## Decision

Frame the paper around the counterfactual that matters economically:

**Does the evidence support the hypothesis that allowing foreign-flag carriers to transport domestic cargo between Honolulu and U.S. West Coast ports would reduce container freight rates?**

## Hypothesis

I expect rates in both directions to fall if the coastwise restriction were removed, with the larger effect likely on the higher-priced westbound leg. The mechanism is not simply “foreign ships are cheaper.” Removing the restriction would expand the feasible carrier set and allow international carriers to combine Asia, Hawaiʻi, and Mainland cargo on the same network, potentially improving vessel utilization and increasing competition.

Today a foreign vessel can carry cargo loaded in Asia to Honolulu and continue to a Mainland port, but it cannot load Hawaiʻi-origin domestic cargo in Honolulu for delivery to the Mainland. Likewise, it cannot load Mainland domestic cargo for Honolulu. Without that restriction, more legs of a transpacific rotation could carry paying cargo.

## Why the Question Is Plausible

Direct foreign service already exists. ONE’s 2026 Asia Hawaii Express runs Pusan → Yokohama → Honolulu → Pusan. Matson’s own CLX integrates Long Beach → Honolulu → Asia → Long Beach, showing that Hawaiʻi can be part of a larger transpacific network.

The domestic market is concentrated: Matson identifies Pasha as its primary Jones Act container competitor. Matson publishes five weekly West Coast departures to Hawaiʻi; Pasha advertises three. High vessel fixed costs also create scale-based barriers even without regulation.

Published Matson tariffs show westbound 40-foot base rates roughly 70% above eastbound rates in 2024–2026. Pasha’s 2014 tariff showed a similar directional ratio. That suggests cargo imbalance and unused backhaul capacity matter greatly.

The distributional question is separate from the rate question. Any higher freight cost may be dispersed across many shippers, retailers, and households, while benefits from restricted entry are concentrated among a smaller set of carriers, maritime labor, shipyards, and related capital owners. That asymmetry may help explain why the policy is durable even if the per-consumer effect is small.

## What Would Prove Me Wrong?

The hypothesis would be weakened if foreign carriers would not enter even after the restriction disappeared because Hawaiʻi’s volume is too small, the Honolulu deviation is too costly, port or vessel constraints are binding, or current domestic service already prices near incremental cost.

## Economic Concepts

Oligopoly and pricing power; barriers to entry; economies of scale; marginal versus average cost; elasticity and substitutes; economic rent; consumer/producer surplus and incidence; and potential national-security externalities.

## Measurement Plan

Use Matson and Pasha tariffs and schedules, foreign-carrier Honolulu services, and available cargo-flow data. The paper should estimate what the evidence supports and clearly separate observed rate differences from the causal effect of the law.
